The NBA's New DNA:
College Money, Economics Speed Up the League's Global Expansion

by Simon Galkevich
May 20, 2026

There was a time when a teenager arriving in the NBA from Moscow via Madrid, with a one-season pitstop in the NCAA, would look like an exotic outlier. But in 2026, Egor Demin, fresh off a rookie campaign with the Brooklyn Nets, stands not as a basketball curiosity, but as the living manifestation of the league’s new DNA.

Once dominated by homegrown talent, the NBA hit historic new highs in the 2025–26 season, with 142 foreign‑born players from 47 countries taking the court. The league’s international share, another record, stayed above the symbolic 25% mark until the regular season’s final weeks.

This demographic shift drives the league's newest initiatives, from the debut of the All-Star 'World vs. USA' format in February to the upcoming launch of NBA Europe, slated for 2027. Furthermore, a foreign star has now hoisted the Most Valuable Player award for eight consecutive years—a streak dating back to James Harden’s win in 2018.

The arrival of 18 new European players anchored a record-breaking year for the NBA’s global talent pool. That group includes Demin and Vladislav Goldin, the first Russian players in the league since 2018. As a result, the total number of Europeans jumped from 63 to 78, marking the largest ever season-to-season growth for international players from a single continent.

Demin and Goldin belong to an exploding wave of overseas athletes capitalizing on the NCAA’s rule changes. In 2021, by allowing athletes to profit from their personal brand (Name, Image, and Likeness, or NIL), giving transfers the freedom to play immediately, and removing barriers for overseas players with professional backgrounds, the NCAA fundamentally redefined college sports.

The same year, for the first time in two decades, foreign players entering the league via the NCAA outnumbered those arriving directly from overseas. This inverted ratio has since solidified into the league's new standard. To put the scale of this movement in perspective, the number of international players with college backgrounds on active NBA rosters has surged by 69% since the 2018–19 season.

“NIL has obviously changed everything,” says Goldin, 25, who played five collegiate seasons before joining the Miami Heat last summer. “The new rules opened another path for international players choosing between NCAA and playing professionally in their countries. I’m sure we will see more players taking a college path.”

“LIKE BEING IN SPACE”

Growing up in Russia, Egor Demin felt a world away from ever stepping onto an NBA court.

"It was really hard to think about the NBA being in Moscow, being in Russia," the 20-year-old Nets guard says. "Because it's really far, it's like being in space. It's just a different world. It was like, 'How can I get there? And I don't see that trajectory. I don't see that path.’”

His path began to materialize in 2021—the same year that, an ocean away, the NCAA entered its “NIL Era”—when Demin, then 15, moved to Spain to join the renowned Real Madrid academy.

Over the next three years, Demin mastered Spanish and English while adjusting, for the first time, to life in a different country. By the end of his second season, he debuted for Real's first team. And like every kid in the academy, Demin set his mind on a dream: to be “like Luka.” Club legend Luka Dončić, an NBA superstar since 2018, once sent the young Russian a signed jersey from Dallas to fuel his ambition.

By 2024, the jump to the newly transformed NCAA felt like the logical next step. BYU offered Demin a chance to develop under Kevin Young, a coach with nearly a decade of NBA experience, not to mention the NIL money a teenager could never earn in Europe.

The move to Utah paid off big time. In the summer of 2025, the Brooklyn Nets selected Demin eighth overall, making him the highest-drafted Russian player in history. He quickly lived up to the hype, earning a Rising Stars selection at the All-Star Weekend and delivering some of the most electric clutch sequences Nets fans have witnessed in years.

“It was an invaluable experience, a completely new feeling,” says Demin, who finished the season due to injury in mid-March after playing 52 games. “Now I know so much more about the league, the teams, and my own potential—and that’s the biggest thing for me.”

Goldin, who signed with Miami after going undrafted last year, established himself by appearing in nine games in his rookie campaign.

“I proved to myself that I can play on this level,” says Goldin. “I proved a lot to myself this season. Maybe even more so during our practice scrimmages, matching up against teammates who play big minutes in the NBA.”

THE BLUEPRINT

International rookies have to adjust not only to the NBA style of play, but also to a hyper-competitive professional environment. For Goldin, having teammates from Italy, Lithuania, Serbia, Sweden on the Heat roster—no team in the league features more Europeans—eased that transition.

“It benefits me that we have many foreigners on the team, because I feel more like I’m one of the guys,” he says.

The modern locker room culture that allows international rookies to feel instantly at home—once a massive barrier for overseas talent—is the fruit of a decades-long effort started by David Stern when he took the helm of the NBA in February 1984.

The new Commissioner envisioned the league—which then had only eight players born outside of the U.S.—as a global brand, and under his watch the barriers began to crumble.

As early as September 1984, the Phoenix Suns, New Jersey Nets, and Seattle Supersonics landed to play exhibition games in Israel, Italy, West Germany and Switzerland. In 1987, the McDonald’s Open debuted—a groundbreaking competition featuring NBA teams against clubs from Europe, South America, and Oceania. A year later, the Atlanta Hawks made history by traveling to the USSR to play three games in the “enemy’s lair.”

But the real “big bang” arrived in April 1989, when the International Basketball Federation (FIBA) allowed NBA players to compete in the Olympics.

The move did more than just send the 1992 “Dream Team” to Barcelona to inspire generations worldwide. It also cleared a path for international stars to join the NBA. Previously, joining the league meant a ban from one’s national team—the very reason European legend Dražen Dalipagić famously turned down the Boston Celtics in 1976.

In the Fall of 1989, the so-called “Green Card Five”—Vlade Divac, Žarko Paspalj, Šarūnas Marčiulionis, Dražen Petrović, and Alexander Volkov—landed in the States in a watershed moment for the sport. In an ironic twist of fate, the transfers of Divac and Paspalj from Partizan Belgrade took place through the club’s manager: Dražen Dalipagić.

After that, the global momentum never faltered. In 1994, Hakeem Olajuwon made history as the first foreign-born MVP. In 2002, Chinese phenom Yao Ming became the first international player drafted No. 1 overall without having played U.S. college basketball. In 2014, the San Antonio Spurs, the longtime gold standard in overseas scouting, won the title with a rotation featuring only two Americans among their top nine players.

“Basketball has become a truly global game, and it’s only natural that it is also reflected in a growing number of international players in the NBA,” says FIBA Europe President Jorge Garbajosa. “Above all, this is a result of the continued evolution and development of the sport – the global talent pool is simply deeper than ever with more and more stars from all around the world.”

The depth of the pool is not an accident. It didn’t take long for Stern to realize that the league had to cast a wider scouting net to maintain its quality. The annual Basketball Without Borders (BWB) camps—launched globally in 2001— rank among his signature achievements. Over the past quarter-century, the program has grown into the ultimate international pipeline, sending 143 former campers to the NBA and WNBA, with more than 50 of them in the league today.

“With players like Jokic, Giannis, Embiid, Luka and now Wemby, there’s more spotlight on the globalization of the NBA,” says Marshall Glickman, former president of the Portland Trail Blazers and the son of the club’s founder. “But because of David Stern, the league was light years ahead of the NFL and other U.S. properties decades ago.”

FOLLOWING THE MONEY

The difference between now and “back then” lies in the NBA’s economics, notes Glickman, whose father traveled with the league’s delegation to the USSR in 1988.

“Today, the highest paid EuroLeague players make €3-4 million, which is the money that the lowest-paid NBA players make, let’s say ninth or tenth players in the rotation,” says Glickman, the former EuroLeague CEO. “The players are quicker to jump to the NBA because of money.”

Indeed, today it’s hard to imagine that the second overall pick of the NBA draft would choose Italy over the league, but this was once a reality. In 1989, Danny Ferry—along with Brian Shaw, who left the Boston Celtics after his rookie year—signed lucrative deals with Il Messaggero Rome.

The NBA is riding an unprecedented commercial surge. The current season kicks off a landmark 11-year, $76 billion media rights deal, delivering a 160% increase in average annual value over the previous agreement. Today, NBA games and programming span 214 countries and territories—nearly tripling its reach since 1992—in more than 50 languages. 

An NBA franchise now averages $5.4 billion in value. In the 2024-25 season, NBA teams generated $12.25 billion in revenue with team sponsorship revenue increasing 91% over the past five years.

Behind these soaring numbers stands the league’s leadership, which recognizes a simple truth: the NBA’s future is global.

“95% of the world’s population is outside the U.S., and in many of those countries, basketball is either the No. 1 or No. 2 sport,” says NBA Senior Vice President, Head of International Strategy & Operations Leah MacNab. “So, by definition our biggest growth opportunities are international.”

THE NEXT FRONTIER

Next on the horizon: the launch of NBA Europe, the second professional league—after the Basketball Africa League, now in its sixth season—run by the Association outside North America. The proposed 16-team tournament targets an October 2027 tip-off.

The league's focus on the Old Continent perfectly aligns with its current talent map, where Europeans account for 55% of all foreign-born players. This contingent led by France, which accounts for one in three Europeans who entered the league this decade—26 out of 76.

“Basketball is the fastest-growing sport in Europe,” says MacNab. “Our goal is to maximize the competitive and commercial potential of basketball on the continent by creating a global league based in Europe that builds on and celebrates Europe’s rich basketball traditions and passionate fanbases while benefiting from the NBA’s league and team operations expertise.”

Marshall Glickman, part of the London bid to join the league, believes NBA Europe “will no doubt move the ecosystem of global basketball commercially.”

“NBA’s thesis is that European basketball is underperforming market potential quite dramatically,” says Glickman. “The NBA believes they can turn a €100 million business [EuroLeague’s current annual revenue] to €300 million in a short time.”

“More than anything, the NBA Europe project is motivated by money,” he adds. “They have been planning this for several years and expect to generate €5 billion in franchise fees in Europe. The NBA will invest big to develop demand in non-traditional markets like London and Rome, which have been overlooked by EuroLeague.”

In the NBA’s way stands the EuroLeague, the continent's premier club competition. According to Glickman, NBA Europe will launch with or without partnering with the EuroLeague, but if the two sides can't find common ground, the debut could be delayed until 2028.

THE CASUALTIES

In April, the NBA announced that 71 players have applied for early entry into the 2026 Draft—the lowest figure since 2003 and the continuation of a steady decline since the introduction of NIL, dropping from 353 entrants in 2021 to 283 in 2022, 242 in 2023, 201 in 2024, and 109 last year. Simply put, for most players, leaving college early is a bad financial decision.

“I absolutely think there’s a possibility that five years from now we’re gonna have a problem in the NBA, because to leave college will mean to lose money for players,” says David B. Thorpe, an NBA player development pioneer. “I’m working with the players right now, who have just signed NIL deals worth over $2 million. You have to be a late first-round pick to make this money in the NBA.”

Thorpe notes that both American and overseas players benefit from NIL deals. In fact, the number of imports in NCAA Men’s Division I has more than doubled since 2010, rising from 406 to 888 last year, with 505 of them coming from Europe.

Five years into the NIL era, Illinois reaching the Final Four on the backs of the ‘Balkan Five’ or Michigan winning a national championship with a starting five made up entirely of transfers, four of them newcomers, surprises no one.

While the NBA may run into some problems down the road, the massive talent drain—with even 23-year-old players, now jumping to NCAA— has already become a serious concern serious concern in Europe.

“Both FIBA and the clubs in Europe were not ready for the NIL, completely not ready,” says Marin Sedlaček, a legendary international scout who worked for the Memphis Grizzlies and Philadelphia 76ers for nearly two decades. “And even when it started five years ago, they were not taking it seriously. So they didn’t find a way to protect themselves.”

Last year, in response to player and stakeholder questions, FIBA published a guide, addressing the changes to the U.S. sports college system.

“We want our young players to be successful, continue to develop and find their place in the sport,” says Garbajosa. “It’s important that we maintain an open-minded and forward-looking approach, but we also cannot ignore the challenges the recent shifts have brought – especially when it comes to the NIL landscape.”

In June, to confront these challenges, Belgrade will host the first “Euro NIL Symposium.”

“Now Europe should find a way to make some regulation,” says Sedlaček, who for a long time served as the operational engine behind BWB camps and Nike Hoop Summits. “And it’s tricky because the NCAA is not a member of FIBA. Who will invest into developing kids, knowing that by the age 18, they will go to NCAA?”

Glickman believes such regulations will feature in the NBA Europe negotiations.

“NIL poses a major threat to the traditional European system,” Glickman says. “We will likely see the academy system decline in importance. And we might see adjustments to how the European clubs that run academies are compensated, given that FIBA is the NBA’s partner.”

A BORDERLESS LEAGUE

The influx of international talent to the U.S. causes anxiety domestically, too. NBA legends like Paul Pierce have been sounding the alarm for years, arguing that flaws in the American training system have led to a “hunger disparity” between domestic prospects and their foreign-born peers.

But no matter the casualties in this developmental war, a further increase in the share of foreign-born players remains beyond question. The only questions now concern how fast and how far that percentage will rise.

“I’m 61, and if by the time I’m 71 the league is 95% international, I couldn’t care less,” Thorpe says. “To say America is suffering because we're letting foreigners play—that just tells me you don't really care about having the best league of talent.”

Glickman expects the share of international players to reach roughly 35% over the next decade. He also doesn’t foresee the NBA ever implementing a domestic player quota—a possibility the league confirms has never been on the table.

“That’s not something we’re considering,” says MacNab. “The NBA has always been built on the principle that talent can come from anywhere, and the best players in the world want to play in the NBA. The strength of the league today is a direct reflection of that approach.”

Which means Egor Demin has a pretty good shot at fulfilling his dream: motivating the next kid somewhere in the world watching him play, just as Luka Dončić once motivated him.

“If I can inspire kids to work super hard and get on this level,” Demin says. “If I can inspire them to believe that it can be real, that would be my best accomplishment over anything else, even over accomplishing my own dream of being an NBA player.”
